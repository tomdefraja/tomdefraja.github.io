Fort William - Knoydart
=====

Day 1
-----
A guilty lunch in Wetherspoons in Fort William and a final glance at the weather forecast, the ferry takes us to the start of the trail on the other side of Loch Linnhe. The walk north starts southbound along the road, and we set off along side a surprising number of Germans and an Irishman, Kevin, all doing the CWT. The 10km trudge on the tarmac with full backpacks (including >8 days of food, 4 litres of sparkling water and 3 bottles of Lidl’s finest Whiskey) makes for a tough start, but a gravel track and a shrine-like collection of painted pebbles heralds the start of the walk in earnest. 

We continue up Cona Glenn, whose south side is full of golden sun and the sound of cuckoos, we run into Kevin's camp, who tells us he aims to walk until the weather turns, he hopes to make it at least to Ullapool. We made camp outside the shut Corrlarach Bothy.

Day 2
-----

Continuing on Cona Glenn, up the pass and to Glenfinnan, we cool off in the Finnan by Corryhully bothy. Over the pass what is usually an endless bog is instead a pleasant descent towards Loch Arkaig.  We camp outside a busy A'Chuil bothy.

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day2" %}

Day 3
-----

After removing a large amount of ticks in the morning (lesson learnt not to go about long grass in sliders and no socks), we make our way over the Bealach and down towards Sourlies, which apparently had over 14 tents out the night before thanks to the TGO coast to coast event. Crossing the estuary, we are entranced by a herd of deer wading out into the sea to cool off. Another fun pass and we approach Inverie, we leave our tent outside the town and headed for the pub, The Old Forge. 
By chance there happens to be a wonderful gig that night, the duo of Catherine MacLellan and Lucy Farrel, and we dine on the out-of-date crisps they are giving away for free.

Knoydart - Ullapool
======

Day 4
-----

Another pass in the morning opens up the next leg of the journey, and we climb down to Barrisdale bay, which gives us our first view of Ladhar Bheinn. We stop and chat at the bothy, joined by a pair of walkers/kayakers and a pair of eels, overlooked by a cruise ship come to see the Loch. The fast moving tides of Loch Hourn means the kayakers race past us as we walk along the coast. A dip in a burn gives our feet respite from the ups and downs, and the path then turns into a stunningly built path right on the loch. We begin the climb up to the pass under the Saddle at around 6 in the evening, and say to ourselves we will stop at the next good campsite and complete the pass in the morning. As the night falls and the path fades, we find ourselves 4 hours older and pitching camp at the bealach, tired, but knowing this was always going to be where we finish. The day feels fulfilled. 

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day4" %}

Day 5
-----

We are wakened by the sunrise in our tent, red light and the moring fog pouring across the mountain side and valley, the crisp air swirling them together in nature's couldron. We make our way down to Shiel bridge and have lunch at The Pitstop by Loch Duich, where we chat to a variety of people: a female trio of Italians visiting the area; a Dutch father and daughter touring the UK in a classic car; and an Icelandic man hiking the TGO coast to coast. Bellies full, we head into Kintail, a remote and isolated area, but whose beauty quickly becomes apparent. Descending towards the Falls of Glomach, there was nothing that could have prepared us for approaching the third largest waterfall in the UK from above. The gorge it carves allows the birch trees to paint the deep valley, to steep for deer. We emerge suddenly into Glen Elchaig, and find camp by Loch na Leitreach.

Day 6
-----

Leaving Glen Elchaig, the track quickly became surrounded by the desolate moorland of Killilan Forest. At the remote Maol Bhuide, the stories of how this bothy was constructed -- complete with images of the builders gave carrying comically large loads of construction material on their back, who would have had to walk at least 10 miles to get there -- reminds us how lucky we are to have such an association as the Mountain Bothies Association. The recent restoration gives the bothy a comfortable feel, and we are tempted to stay the night. Instead, we cut across the moorland to the path, and cool off in Loch Calavie. We decide that we will detour from the recommended route, relying on dry spring to allow us to wade across the River Carron, either tonight or the next day. The bog to Bearnais bothy -- or lack thereof -- reminds us just how dry it is, and that the Carron crossing is not out realms of possibility. At the bothy, we chat to a Glaswegian who is returning to the bothy for the first time in 40 years. At the pass, we decide to camp and cross the Carron tomorrow. Though we have to collect cooking water from a stagnant bog, the views make this worth it. To the north, Torridon towers. West and Southwest the islands of the Outer Hebrides and Skye fade into the haze. To the south, familiar faces show themselves, The Saddle and Ladhar Bheinn. The sunset dazzles us with colours, clouds roll away beneath us.

Day 7
------

The clouds the night before having left their mark on our camp, we pack the wet tent away and head down to cross the River Carron. Across the river, we meet a couple getting off at the Achnashellach the train station, who match our pace entering the Coulin Forest. Walking through Glen Lair after cooling in the river, we are fascinated by its geology, suddenly switching between sandstone, quartzite and gneiss formations. We cool off once again in the Lochan at the top of the pass, before rounding the corner and being greeted by the wall of Liathach and Beinn Eighe in Torridon. The awe of the south face only increases as we approach, and we make our way up the glen between these two giants. Again, we ruminate on the fascinating geology of the place (Conor's girlfriend being an geologist, we fancy ourselves experts on the subject). As we round the final corner, looking out onto the moors and peaks of Torridon, we know we are going to witness another special sunset. We race up the last climb to catch the last of the sun on the loch as we jump in its cold water, overseen by the towering triple butress of Beinn Eighe. We give first aid to a mountaineer who fell on the way up, there to photograph some climbers the next day. We are stunned by the bravery of a man also camping there, with a leg condition causing him serious pain when walking. The pass that took us 2 hours took him 7. He is training to walk the Cape Wrath trail. We drink whiskey and watch the sun set.
