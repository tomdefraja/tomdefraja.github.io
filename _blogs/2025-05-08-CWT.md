Fort William - Knoydart
=====

Day 1
-----
A guilty lunch in Wetherspoons in Fort William and a final glance at the weather forecast, the ferry takes us to the start of the trail on the other side of Loch Linnhe. The walk north starts southbound along the road, and we set off along side a surprising number of Germans and an Irishman, Kevin, all doing the CWT. The 10km trudge on the tarmac with full backpacks (including >8 days of food, 4 litres of sparkling water and 3 bottles of Lidl’s finest Whiskey) makes for a tough start, but a gravel track and a shrine-like collection of painted pebbles heralds the start of the walk in earnest. 

We continue up Cona Glenn, whose south side is full of golden sun and the sound of cuckoos, we run into Kevin's camp, who tells us he aims to walk until the weather turns, he hopes to make it at least to Ullapool. We made camp outside the shut Corrlarach Bothy.

Day 2
-----

Continuing on Cona Glenn, up the pass and to Glenfinnan, we cool off in the Finnan by Corryhully bothy. Over the pass what is usually an endless bog is instead a pleasant descent towards Loch Arkaig.  We camp outside a busy A'Chuil bothy.

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day2" %}

Day 3
-----

After removing a large amount of ticks in the morning (lesson learnt not to go about long grass in sliders and no socks), we make our way over the Bealach and down towards Sourlies, which apparently had over 14 tents out the night before thanks to the TGO coast to coast event. Crossing the estuary, we are entranced by a herd of deer wading out into the sea to cool off. Another fun pass and we approach Inverie, we leave our tent outside the town and headed for the pub, The Old Forge. 
By chance there happens to be a wonderful gig that night, the duo of Catherine MacLellan and Lucy Farrel, and we dine on the out-of-date crisps they are giving away for free.

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day3" %}

Knoydart - Ullapool
======

Day 4
-----

Another pass in the morning opens up the next leg of the journey, and we climb down to Barrisdale bay, which gives us our first view of Ladhar Bheinn. We stop and chat at the bothy, joined by a pair of walkers/kayakers and a pair of eels, overlooked by a cruise ship come to see the Loch. The fast moving tides of Loch Hourn means the kayakers race past us as we walk along the coast. A dip in a burn gives our feet respite from the ups and downs, and the path then turns into a stunningly built path right on the loch. We begin the climb up to the pass under the Saddle at around 6 in the evening, and say to ourselves we will stop at the next good campsite and complete the pass in the morning. As the night falls and the path fades, we find ourselves 4 hours older and pitching camp at the bealach, tired, but knowing this was always going to be where we finish. The day feels fulfilled. 

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day4" %}

Day 5
-----

We are wakened by the sunrise in our tent, red light and the moring fog pouring across the mountain side and valley, the crisp air swirling them together in nature's couldron. We make our way down to Shiel bridge and have lunch at The Pitstop by Loch Duich, where we chat to a variety of people: a female trio of Italians visiting the area; a Dutch father and daughter touring the UK in a classic car; and an Icelandic man hiking the TGO coast to coast. Bellies full, we head into Kintail, a remote and isolated area, but whose beauty quickly becomes apparent. Descending towards the Falls of Glomach, there was nothing that could have prepared us for approaching the third largest waterfall in the UK from above. The gorge it carves allows the birch trees to paint the deep valley, to steep for deer. We emerge suddenly into Glen Elchaig, and find camp by Loch na Leitreach. 

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day5" %}

Day 6
-----

Leaving Glen Elchaig, the track quickly became surrounded by the desolate moorland of Killilan Forest. At the remote Maol Bhuide, the stories of how this bothy was constructed -- complete with images of the builders gave carrying comically large loads of construction material on their back, who would have had to walk at least 10 miles to get there -- reminds us how lucky we are to have such an association as the Mountain Bothies Association. The recent restoration gives the bothy a comfortable feel, and we are tempted to stay the night. Instead, we cut across the moorland to the path, and cool off in Loch Calavie. We decide that we will detour from the recommended route, relying on dry spring to allow us to wade across the River Carron, either tonight or the next day. The bog to Bearnais bothy -- or lack thereof -- reminds us just how dry it is, and that the Carron crossing is not out realms of possibility. At the bothy, we chat to a Glaswegian who is returning to the bothy for the first time in 40 years. At the pass, we decide to camp and cross the Carron tomorrow. Though we have to collect cooking water from a stagnant bog, the views make this worth it. To the north, Torridon towers. West and Southwest the islands of the Outer Hebrides and Skye fade into the haze. To the south, familiar faces show themselves, The Saddle and Ladhar Bheinn. The sunset dazzles us with colours, clouds roll away beneath us.

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day6" %}

Day 7
------

The clouds the night before having left their mark on our camp, we pack the wet tent away and head down to cross the River Carron. Across the river, we meet a couple getting off at the Achnashellach the train station, who match our pace entering the Coulin Forest. Walking through Glen Lair after cooling in the river, we are fascinated by its geology, suddenly switching between sandstone, quartzite and gneiss formations. We cool off once again in the Lochan at the top of the pass. We run into the Thistly Tours group who are also doing the Cape Wrath Trail. Rounding the corner, we are greeted by the wall of Liathach and Beinn Eighe in Torridon. The awe of the south face only increases as we approach, and we make our way up the glen between these two giants. Again, we ruminate on the fascinating geology of the place (Conor's girlfriend being an geologist, we fancy ourselves experts on the subject). As we round the final corner, looking out onto the moors and peaks of Torridon, we know we are going to witness another special sunset. We race up the last climb to catch the last of the sun on the loch as we jump in its cold water, overseen by the towering triple butress of Beinn Eighe. We give first aid to a mountaineer who fell on the way up, there to photograph some climbers the next day. We are stunned by the bravery of a man also camping there, with a leg condition causing him serious pain when walking. The pass that took us 2 hours took him 7. He is training to walk the Cape Wrath trail. We drink whiskey and watch the sun set.


{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day7" %}

Day 8
------

The path leading down from the Loch quickly disappears. The landscape feels desolate and progress is slow. Overhead we see the cuckoos we have been hearing since day one. As we climb the pass the moor is overcome by rock coming off the imposing Beinn Eighe, but we quickly spot Kinlochewe below surrounded by green. We refuel with 2 jacket potatoes each and plenty of snacks, and chat once again with the Thistly Tours group. We set off somewhat lethargically, and reach Lochan Fada. The beach on the loch faces Fisherfield Forest, which we gaze over as we swim. In evening light the sky comes alive with colour, and I am reminded of the paintings of my grandfather, Clifford Fishwick. 

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day8" %}

Day 9 & 10
------

Leaving the loch behind, we come across a plunge pool, which despite us wanting to make decent progress on the day, keeps us in its waters for a while. The following glen gives views of Bidein a' Ghlas Thuill, which watches us closely for the rest of the day. We reach the road for Ullapool, and decide against walking along in to the town, getting a taxi instead. We are told of a vintage motorbike festival happening in town, and discover many of the participants at a Jonny Cash tribute act in the hotel. We stay until late, stumbling up the hill in the early morning hours to make camp. We spend much of the following day recovering and resupplying and enjoying the sun in Ullapool. We walk in the waning light just outside the town and set up camp near Loch Achall.

Ullapool - Cape Wrath
=======

Day 11
-------

We follow a gravel track for the majority of the day, which means quick but painful progress on our feet. We stop at Knockdamph bothy for a snack, and The Schoolhouse bothy for lunch, which, as its name suggests, was used as a school for a handful of kids in the area, and included a room for the teacher to live in. We swim in the river nearby, and then warm our feet on the sun heated metal of the bothy and munch on carrots. At the River Oykel hotel, a man staying there for the fishing complains that it has been the driest spring since 1964. Apparently the weather does not make for good fishing. We do not complain, as it makes for good walking. We camp alongside the Oykel after a long day.

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day10" %}

Day 12
-------

Our slow morning routine is interrupted by the energetic couple we saw getting off the train at Achnashellach, who stayed in a fishing hut the nearby, and we delight our paths crossed again. A woman living at Benmore lodge feeds us ice cream, which keeps us going for the detour we have planned. We decide to climb Ben More Assynt from the south, which involves some ridge scrambling. Unsure of the severity of the ridge, we make our way up the steep slope to the start. A few exposed climbs and narrow ridges later, and we enjoy a whiskey two meters short of a thousand meters in altitude. No mountain nearby is taller, and this gives us staggering views in all directions. To the north, the rocky Gneiss of Sutherland, and the northern coastline we are aiming for. To the west, the isolated peaks of Assynt; Canisp and the wall of Suilven, and further out the Outer Hebrides. To the east, Loch Shin like a tear the flat canvas of moorland out toward the east coast. South, the path we have walked and mountains we have seen are hard to make out, but their presence is unmistakable. Torridon, Skye and Ladhar Bheinn make themselves known, and even the Cairngorms make an unexpected visit. The cloud rolls over the still evening, and we don the fly sheet for the first time just in time for the wind to pick up.

Day 13
-------

The endless views persist in the morning as we descend, the heat intesifying. Cooling off in a pool in the valley, we again run into the leader of Thistly tours, on his way to meet the rest of the group. Another long pass and descent and we round the corner to the tallest waterfall in the UK, Eas a' Chuil Alainn, which has been reduced to a, admittedly very long, trickle in the dry weather. Loch Glencoul gives spectacular views however, and we sleep and share whiskey with others in the Glencoul bothy.

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day12" %}

Day 14
-------

We climb and round the hill beside Loch Glencoul, the morning sun hot as we watch the days workers arrive by boat to work on the bothy. Loch Glencoul becomes Loch Glendhu, and we rest and swim at the bothy of the latter. The coastal paths quickly rises up to Ben Dreavie, and the mountains of Ben Stack, Arkle and Foinaven dominate to the north. There is a curious geology atop Ben Dreavie, pebbledash-like and perfectly flat and level rocks seem like ancient foundations, the only such rocks we encounter amongst the gneiss of the north. Over the river Laxford, and we make our way past Arkle, and encounter the couple once again camped on a loch's beach. We swim and camp by the following loch after a big days walking.

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day13" %}

Day 15
-------

We look forward to a calmer day, and we earn our double pitstop in Kinlochbervie after a long walk on asphalt. The first stop at the iconic London Stores. The second at the local Spar, where we encounter both the couple and the Thistly tours group. We finally decide to make our way to Sandwood bay, in time to enjoy the beach to ourselves for the evening. The pristine waters, white sands and spiky machir tempt us to rest in this place the following day.

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day14" %}

Day 16
-------

The day starts with egg and bacon rolls for breakfast cooked on the beach and quick swim behind the rocks. A makeshift sauna from the fly sheet of the tent and the sunlight for heat, together with a kettle inside, and we spend much of the day in and out of the water. We await the Thistly tours group who are coming down from Cape Wrath. We spot them coming and make our way over, greeted by the hugs of Jean and her endless praise. We feel the praise unfounded from her, being 76 and having walked much of the Cape Wrath after twisting her ankle early on! As we leave, we cheer on a son and father who have just caught a fish in the sea. As we near Strathchailleach bothy, our first rain of the trip starts to fall. We tell Hans, a German on the trail, of our plans the following day. The peat fire warms the room, surrounded by the murals painted by the hermit of Strathchailleach,

Day 17
-------

In the morning, we take a bearing of 355 degrees making for the lighthouse, and pack away all maps and phones, to attempt to walk the final stretch in as straight a line as possible. Having told Hans our plans, an engineer in his early 60s, he seems excited to join us on our challenge. Despite the dryness of the season, the straight line leads us through waist deep bog and steep wet slopes. Despite the low fog, the barren moorland provides good visibility, and as we round the last hill the lighthouse of Cape Wrath appear, and at precisely 355 degrees! Chuffed with our navigational luck, we enjoy a big lunch at the Ozone Cafe. We sign the guestbook, and see that our Irish friend Kevin from day one had passed through a few days before us. Having reached the north, we make our way east, to stay at the spectacular Kervaig bothy. We swim and watch the puffins before the storm comes in. The fire sparks jovial conversation with the rest of the bothy stayers, including our good friends the couple Jimmy and Ken. 

{% include carousel.html carousel_file="CWT" carousel_id="Day16" %}

Day 18
-------

We leave the bothy with the plan to walk round the Kyle of Durness, the ferry we have been told will be reserved for the runners completing the Cape Wrath ultra. The sprawling kyle with seals sat on the low-tide sands tempts us to wade across, something Jimmy had talked about the night before but we had dismissed due to the storm rolling in. In the end, we wade across slightly further up stream, and get a lift to Durness with an Austrian man and dog in their camper. We set up camp in the campsite, and enjoy our first shower since the start of the walk. We walk along the road, past the John Lennon memorial, before finally reaching The Smoo Cave, and our journey feels complete. The storm rolls in and we enjoy a big meal in the shelter of the kitchen. The next morning, we get the morning bus and start the journey home.
