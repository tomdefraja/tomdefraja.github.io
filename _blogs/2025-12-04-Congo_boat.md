Oyo - Mossaka
---

Just as we are leaving the town of Oyo, we cross the river, a tributary of a tributary of the Congo river, and plans we had to take a trip down the river resurface. Through speaking to a police man nearby, he gives us the number of the chief of police for the fishing town of Mossaka, only accessible by boat, and the starting point of a ship to the capital Brazzaville. The chief of police tells Antoine there is a boat leaving in 3 days, so we rush back and head to the port of Oyo. There, we see workers loading a pirogue with jerry cans of petrol [1] leaving for Liranga, and stopping at Mossaka. We offer help and a beer to the workers, our intentions quite clear by now, and after speaking to the captain Jonas, he offers us passage to Mossaka for a little cake [2], and a coke to sweeten the deal. 

The bikes loaded, like sparse sprinkling on the layers of jerry cans, sacks of materials and manioc that form the bulk of the boat, we set off. A glorious sunset turns into an intense thunderstorm, but with nowhere to evacuate we improvise a shelter using the fly of my tent strapped to the bikes. The wind-inflated shelter works remarkably well, much to the amusement of captain Jonas, as he says to a trio of travellers on the boat that “les blancs construit leur maison en cinq minutes”. He comes over to inspect our handiwork, and our worries of potential capsize are but to bed when he says he has been doing this trip for 27 years. The trio, who clearly have experience of making this trip, as they lay on their mattresses framed by the canisters of petrol under a perfectly sized tarp, are are also highly amused by our engineering, and remark to Jonas as he returns to stern that “les blancs restent les blancs”. We, like them, stay dry, enough even to do some reading under torch light as the deluge runs its course into the night.

The poor sods whose groundsheet we donated do not paint the same picture of competence as the trio do, nor the picture of dryness we do, who ask us to take their cell phones to keep dry, and have to be shouted to stop smoking while sitting on the approximately 300 canisters of petrol, the air constantly thick with the smell of it. A couple of hours in, during a lull in the rain, the lightning still machine gunning, and our tent now all but dry from the wind, Antoine goes to check up on the pair. In the Congo and much of western and central Africa, the question “Ça va?” is usually met by one of two answers: a repeat of the question turned statement, “Ça va.”, meaning “all good”, indicating that they are in in moderate to good spirits, and perhaps wanting to engage in pleasant conversation; alternatively, a “pas” may be appended to the initial response, meaning “not good”, signifying they are in bad spirits, or at least they have judged their situation is worse than yours, and the conversation may lead to them wanting something from you. In this case, when Antoine asked how they are, the lightning flashes revealing them huddled together in a puddle of water between barrels of petrol, with the measly tent footprint blowing in the wind so much it only reasonably covered the hands clutching it, this latter response was appended by a further “du tout”, indicating the most dire of situations. 

The sun begins to rise as we turn onto the Congo river for the first time, a complex labyrinth of islands and reeds at points over 14km across, on which we hug the left most wall. Occasionally, an island is large enough to hold houses on stilts, which the locals use to sometimes live and fish in. As the light comes in, the activity on the river on which we will be spending the next week or so increases, and the ripples of our boat are joined by those who live here.

Mossaka
---

We arrive in Mossaka, greeted by those with nothing better to do than watch the loading and loading of the boats [3]. Off the boat, while looking for a place to stay, we find ourselves at the mayors residence and a crowd of people, with flags of the PCT adorning every wall and motorbike, with processions of motorbikes passing every so often. We had stumbled into a party parade, and were now being led (though we are not sure why) into the mayors office to meet with him. 

We are seated across from him at his splendid desk, he is imposing and his face is not one that lightly gives out a smile. He asks us what is it we want, and unsure what to say we fumble out a nervous answer that we wanted to make ourselves known as tourists and thank the mayor for letting us stay in his town. Satisfied, he lets us go, and we speak to his secretary, who is thankfully a much more jovial, and he invites us for drinks later, along with the treasurer and our friend the chief of police (with whom we spoke on the phone). We learn that the mayor and the town was in mourning for the loss of his son. The secretary shows us the highlights of the town, and two days later the chief of police organises for us to speak to the captain of the boat to Brazzaville to secure our passage, hinting that we may have a cabin to ourselves.

As the ferry pulls into port, we realise that this will not be the luxury cabin experience that was suggested. Instead, every inch of the deck is covered in cargo, from a green and golden mountain of plantain — the first structure we see being the tallest — to a sea of buckets full of catfish. More mountains, this time of baby crocodiles, seemingly dead but being kept just alive. Goat pens act as sponges for animals, seeming to be at saturation point with goats but able to fulfil the demands as more and more goats and pigs are loaded into them. The goats do not seem to protest too much, other than their wail as they fly 5 meters through the air into a canoe or a pen, but the pig’s screams are constant as they are dragged by their ears or their rope collar onto the boat, or out of the water they have accidentally been dropped into.

Every other inch is covered by humans, strewn on the floor and covered in tarps after the most recent storm. The structure of the boat, called La paix is hard to ascertain, but roughly it consists of an engine section, about 10 meters long, with a small captain’s cabin, in front of which is a platform 2 meters across and 5 meters long containing the engine, with small gangways below it on either side. In front of this is the main section, which is a large barge roughly 30m long connected to the engine section by a tangle of steel cables. Atop the barge, there has been erected wooden scaffoldings which are used to contain the people and cargo. These we assume are the cabins we had been teased, are usually 2 meters tall, with a platform for say a family to sleep in, and a platform on top that another group could sleep on. There is space for cargo underneath too, ranging from goats or crocodiles or catfish to plantains or yams or chickens. Cargo will also spill over onto the sleeping platforms more often than not. From the outside, the barge seems like a solid mass of scaffolding that one could not possibly move through, but when on the boat one sees that the cabins are separated by a narrow street. Therefore the boat is more of a floating village or market: indeed most people are on the boat to sell their goods in the capital, but they will also sell en route to those on board. Thus, from the engine section, after navigating their way around the mountain of plantain, one can wonder up the street and buy food for the journey. 

As our bikes are whisked away to some unknown location (which we learn later is a mostly empty cargo compartment below the deck of the barge) we are led past the cabins and shown a place on the platform above the engine, where we are told to put our tent up. We ask if any of the cabins are available for us, but he says that he wants us to have a view of both sides of the river. We settle in, watching as cargo is loaded from shore to pirogue to our boat, sat in the little space behind our tent and group of 4 all on the platform, and then asked to move as another group need the space to sleep.

We wait and wait to depart, apparently awaiting the arrival of another boat to arrive before we can depart. The stars shine through the humid air, Orion rises and somersaults through the sky, an ominous red glow shines down the river, I watch expecting it to be the light of the boat, but slowly the moon takes its place, and I drift off to sleep. In the early hours of the morning, after Orion has completed his acrobatics, the slow chugging of the engine shakes me awake, and I watch for almost an hour as the newly arrived boat is being tied to ours, all as we slowly plod downstream.

In the morning, we see that the boat had not been tied to ours, but ours to the new boat, it being more than twice the size of ours. The now fully assembled boat has 3 barges, our original barge and two more in parallel, with the central barge slightly larger than the one we first boarded, and the third a similar size. The barges have similar layouts with a main street running up the spine, though the central barge is big enough such that the street has offshoots and alleyways containing the mass of cabins. The cabins are just as packed with cargo and people, which overflow onto the street, so that one has to hurdle goats in order to progress up the main street. The central barge has an engine section even larger than the one we are sat on, bringing the total number of engines on the ship to two. Since the barges are wider than the engine sections, there is a space between them where the river flows underneath. The barges thus create a horseshoe, making for excellent people watching. The central barge does have one permanent structure, a toilet block, which forms part of the horseshoe, and is vehemently defended by a group of immaculately dressed women, who spend almost the entire journey collecting water from the river in small buckets on a string, transferring this into larger buckets to then wash their clothes in. The outlet pipe of the water sits directly below the women, resulting in a rather unpleasant-to-watch-and-high-stakes-game of what gets scooped out of the water, which from our vantage point every detail can be seen in excruciating detail.

Between short runs to get beers, we mostly sit and watch all that is going on around us. The constant current of women coming and going to wash their clothes, most coming back at least 2 or 3 times a day; the men at the back of the engine section who have set up a barber shop, using only razors to shave heads and beards, unperturbed by the vibrations from the engine; the small canoes that swarm around the boat, forming a solid floor inside horseshoe, locals of passing villages come to sell their fruit. It is mesmerising watch, on only a small corner of this boat. We only come to appreciate the full scale when, after being let onto the roof of the captains deck in exchange for a bottle of whiskey, we can observe the whole boat, glimpsing the people between the flapping of their tarps. We sit on a carpet of peanuts left out to dry, watching the sun set as we float downstream, passing the bottle around to the crew that joined us. The serenity is punctuated by the shouts back on the boat, and on this island of calm and beauty I am reminded that for almost all the passengers on the ship, this is their subsistence.

The next day, the final of this journey, the boat makes its first stops. The only village around is on the DRC side of the river, though the boat does not pull up to a jetty there as one might expect. Instead, the boat all but crashes into the reeds on the opposite bank, still a good 2km away, and the swarm of canoes returns, this time here to offload not only cargo but also people. They board the ship, hurrying the loot onto their boats, before jumping back and rushing off, letting the next boat in to perform this organised piracy. One of the young lad we are taking pictures of (at their request) runs on board to offer us one of the cigar sized blunts they have all been smoking, which we politely decline. He runs back to his boat and manoeuvres it at frightening speed around to where they begin their work of offloading. 3 hours of seems to be all it takes for the offloading, and we set off making for Brazzaville.

The large twin towers of the Hilton Hotel standing sentinel are the first glimpses of our destination that we get, the rest of the skyline appearing deeper into the heart of the city’s shore. The faint skyline of Kinshasa slowly emerges from the smog, as if an army has just crested the hill of the field of battle. Sitting on the boat and seeing the cities face off against one another, one feels the weight of history.

Once we arrive at the port, our own disembarkation is no less hectic and inefficient as the previous. There is no jetty once again, only a steeply angled bank, adorned with an enormous crowd who seem only to have come to watch the pandemonium unfold.  We manage to hop off with our luggage on the thin wooden planks that bridge from the boat to the crowd, and safely store it at the passport control. The question is now, where are our bikes? We jump back on, and search for anyone who looks remotely like crew to tell us where our bikes are. We are told they are in the cargo hold, and after some heated negotiating with the owner of the catfish and chickens that were on the hatch, we are finally reunited with our bikes. I jump down, passing one bike to Antoine and the other to someone who I hope is trying to help. We leave the boat for a second time, though this time the plank is not available to us, meaning we have to jump down to the base of the bank, and climb up the steep slope. The climb, which would have been tricky without hauling a bike up, is accompanied by jeers from the crowd anytime we slightly lose our footing. They help is up the final lip of the bank, and we, our bikes and all we carry make it safely ashore.
